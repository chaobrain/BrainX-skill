## Main findings

The strongest working explanation is a loss of inhibitory control in a recurrent circuit. Activating an inhibitory receptor in an interneuron reduces inhibition of its targets; receptor sign does not determine the sign of the network response. Syn expression also does not imply equal suppression across excitatory and inhibitory cells, layers, axons, or regions. Residual excitatory drive and recurrent amplification can outlast the reduction in inhibitory restraint.

The regional contrast leaves two distinct uncertainties: whether the effective E/I perturbation differs between S1 and S2, and whether similar perturbations encounter different circuit stability margins. Both remain plausible. A downstream inhibitory gate or returning corticocortical/thalamocortical feedback may participate; S2 as a manipulation site does not establish it as the first seizure-generating site.

## Has the question been resolved?

No direct subsequent mechanistic resolution was found through 2026-09-07. Europe PMC returned zero indexed citing works for PMID 41962964; the focused post-publication search returned only that report. The report explicitly retains alternative local and distributed explanations. This search cannot exclude unindexed or unpublished work.

Wu's 2025 preprint precedes the matching 2026 publication. It is related evidence, not a subsequent resolution of that experiment. Its discussion itself retains multiple mechanisms. In particular, suppression of excitation is not a measurement of increased inhibition, and observed epileptiform activity does not establish the abstract's proposed inhibitory dominance.

## Competing explanations and ranking

1. **Local disinhibition, highest priority.** Inhibitory-cell firing and/or GABA output is suppressed enough that residual excitation becomes unstable. PV, SST and other inhibitory subclasses are candidates; neither subtype identity nor a specific S2 enrichment is established. Suppressing VIP alone could instead disinhibit other interneurons, so all GABAergic neurons cannot be treated as one functional class in the follow-up experiment.
2. **Different circuit susceptibility versus different effective targeting, unresolved within the leading explanation.** The same loss of restraint may destabilize S2 but leave S1 stable, or Syn may affect different effective populations in the two areas. Receptor expression, layer coverage, coupling to effectors, release suppression, injected volume and surviving untreated cells all matter. More S2 recurrence is a model hypothesis, not an established anatomical finding here.
3. **Distributed disinhibition or feedback, plausible but constrained.** S2 output may recruit inhibition elsewhere, and remote activity may return to the local circuit. A simple loss of excitatory S2 output should also occur under an equally effective CaMKIIalpha intervention, so that mechanism alone struggles with the negative control. Different projection/layer coverage or combined local inhibitory loss could overcome this constraint, but must be measured.
4. **Suppression-induced synchronization or compensatory excitability, lower priority for this precise contrast.** Reduced excitation can move a nonlinear network into an oscillatory regime; hyperpolarization-related rebound, cAMP/HCN modulation, thalamic burst recruitment or homeostatic change could contribute. No S2-specific evidence currently chooses among these. hM4Di is a signaling perturbation, not an ideal negative-current source. The timing and cell-type controls determine whether detailed channel or adaptation models are warranted.
5. **Intervention confounds.** Off-target expression, spread to neighboring cortex, unequal effective suppression, ligand effects and non-epileptic abnormal movement remain empirical alternatives. Regional/promoter specificity weakens a simple systemic drug explanation without eliminating drug-by-circuit interactions. Use ligand-only and vector-only controls and verify the first electrographic event before assigning ictogenesis to S2.
